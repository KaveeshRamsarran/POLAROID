# POLAROID: anthology concepts

Each episode follows a different person who has a believable reason to carry an instant camera. Keep the tactile camera, limited film, noisy shutter, developing prints, and the contradiction between what the player sees and what the photograph records. Change what a photograph means in each story, the player's ordinary job, and the threat's behaviour.

The Observer can belong to Blackwood House. Every subsequent episode does not need the same creature or another four-photo ritual. Some stories could leave the threat human and make the camera the only supernatural element.

## 1. Vacancy — a roadside motel

You cover a relative's overnight reception shift during a storm. The owner insists that you photograph each room after checkout to document damage. One print shows luggage and a sleeping guest in a room you just emptied. Another shows your own reception desk from inside a locked bathroom.

**Camera rule:** photographs reveal who last occupied a space, but apparently recent images sometimes show events that have not happened yet. Compare prints against the guest register, phone calls, keys, and the actual rooms before deciding whom to admit or which door to unlock.

**Distinctive sequence:** a guest asks you to photograph them for a missing-person poster. Their face is absent from the developing print; a person standing behind you is perfectly exposed.

**Small first build:** reception, a utility room, a parking court, and six accessible rooms in one motel wing. Begin with check-ins, towels, and a broken vending machine before introducing the first impossible photograph. This is the strongest next prototype: it supports ordinary conversations and escalating suspicion while reusing much of the existing door, room, inventory, and interaction work.

## 2. Closing Time — a supermarket

You are a temporary worker documenting shelf damage after closing. The shop radio continues making customer announcements even after the manager confirms that everybody has left. A photograph of the back aisle contains an extra set of shelves and a staff member who is not on the rota.

**Camera rule:** prints reveal the store's previous layout. Align old signage and shelf landmarks to locate sealed stockroom doors and work out where someone can move behind the walls. The flash briefly lights a whole aisle, giving away your location across the store.

**Distinctive sequence:** you photograph a convex security mirror. While the print develops, shopping-cart wheels stop just outside your hiding place. The photograph lets you determine which aisle is actually clear.

**Scope:** a few connected aisles, checkout, freezer, loading bay, and staff office. The threat navigates around shelves and listens for the shutter; there is no basement ritual.

## 3. The Last Crossing — an overnight ferry

You are the deckhand photographing vehicle damage before departure. The passenger count is correct until a power failure. The terminal is no longer visible, and the crew refuses to acknowledge a car that appears in every deck photograph.

**Camera rule:** a photograph records the correct number of occupants, including people absent to the naked eye. Compare images with the manifest to locate an unaccounted-for passenger. Photographing the same window from two sides establishes where the presence really is.

**Distinctive sequence:** a developing print shows somebody inside your locked cabin. You are holding its only key.

**Scope:** vehicle deck, lounge, stairwell, several cabins, and bridge. Rain and engine vibration provide cover for movement. Keep the ferry route scripted to avoid building a sailing simulation.

## 4. Mile 47 — a winter roadside service station

Your car fails near a closed mountain service station. A tow operator tells you to wait indoors and document the vehicle before it is collected. Your first photograph contains footprints approaching the car; there are none in the snow outside.

**Camera rule:** photographs show short, inconsistent glimpses of what will happen next. An open gate, a broken window, or an extinguished light can be a warning that the player still has time to prevent. Developing time matters because you must decide whether to stay exposed or move before the image becomes legible.

**Distinctive sequence:** a print shows your car leaving the forecourt with someone in the passenger seat. Your keys are still in your hand.

**Scope:** station shop, workshop, short roadside stretch, and a maintenance cabin. Concentrate on a few readable landmarks instead of a large explorable forest.

## 5. The Last Showing — a small-town cinema

You help catalogue a cinema before its demolition. The ticket machine prints a sale during the night, followed by a seat number. Taking a photograph from the projection booth reveals an audience in the empty auditorium.

**Camera rule:** photographs expose moments preserved in different film reels. Photographing from the right seat and angle reveals missing actions in an old incident. Every exposure changes where one audience member is sitting; its route through the rows matters more than a conventional chase.

**Distinctive sequence:** your newest print shows the projection-booth window from a seat behind the camera's actual viewpoint.

**Scope:** lobby, auditorium, projection booth, backstage corridor, and archive. Use the screen for environmental clues and silhouettes rather than expensive cutscenes.

## 6. Low Tide — an isolated lighthouse station

You arrive to photograph storm damage for an insurance survey. The local boat operator says the station has been unmanned for years, but there is fresh food in the keeper's kitchen and a lamp that someone keeps relighting.

**Camera rule:** photographs reveal the station at a different tide. A submerged path may be visible in a print, while a room that seems safe appears flooded. Match tide marks, light sweeps, and old photographs to find a route back to the boat.

**Distinctive sequence:** the sea in a developed image is completely still except for one human-shaped wake travelling toward the landing.

**Scope:** keeper's cottage, lighthouse staircase, generator shed, and one short causeway. The exterior provides a strong visual departure from Blackwood House without requiring an open world.

## A light connection between episodes

The camera can pass through an estate sale, a pawnshop, a motel lost-property drawer, or an evidence locker. A recurring scratch on the body and an occasional date on an impossible print establish the connection. Let each protagonist's story resolve on its own; keep the camera's origin ambiguous until there is a reason to explore it.

For the next prototype, build only Vacancy's opening shift and one impossible room photograph. Test whether players enjoy checking the physical room against a developing print before committing to a full second episode.
